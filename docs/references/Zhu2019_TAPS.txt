ABSTRACT
Locating the minimum free energy paths (MFEPs) between two conformational states is among the most important tasks of biomolecular
simulations. For example, knowledge of the MFEP is critical for focusing the effort of unbiased simulations that are used for the construc-
tion of Markov state models to the biologically relevant regions of the system. Typically, existing path searching methods perform local
sampling around the path nodes in a pre-selected collective variable (CV) space to allow a gradual downhill evolution of the path toward
the MFEP. Despite the wide application of such a strategy, the gradual path evolution and the non-trivial a priori choice of CVs are also
limiting its overall efﬁciency and automation. Here we demonstrate that non-local perpendicular sampling can be pursued to accelerate the
search, provided that all nodes are reordered thereafter via a traveling-salesman scheme. Moreover, path-CVs can be computed on-the-ﬂy
and used as a coordinate system, minimizing the necessary prior knowledge about the system. Our traveling-salesman based automated path
searching method achieves a 5-8 times speedup over the string method with swarms-of-trajectories for two peptide systems in vacuum and
solution, making it a promising method for obtaining initial pathways when investigating functional conformational changes between a pair of
structures.

INTRODUCTION
Characterizing the most probable transitions among the stable
conformational states is a crucial step toward the understanding of
the dynamics and function of biomolecules. This is, however, often
impractical for conventional Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations
to achieve because the transitions could involve numerous coordi-
nates that are unknown a priori and high barriers that prohibit the
transition events to occur within simulations of limited length.1
Among the methods devised to tackle these challenges, path
sampling uses a large scale unbiased sampling to obtain an ensemble
of transition paths. 2 For example, the most established path sam-
pling method—transition path sampling 3—and its derivatives 4–8
aim to collect a “complete” ensemble of reactive pathways deﬁned
directly in the phase space and compute the reaction rate. The
degrees of freedom most relevant to the transitions are extracted
from the path ensemble through the transition path theory9 or other
types of post-analysis.10,11

The probable transition paths can also be derived from a
kinetic network model, e.g., Markov State Model (MSM), 12–20 of
the system. 9 Typically, an MSM is constructed from a large set of
unbiased MD trajectories initiated from different locations of the
phase space. The continuous dynamics covered by these trajecto-
ries are then modeled as Markovian transitions among metastable
conformational states at discrete time intervals. Such a feature
allows for highly parallelized sampling and a systematic descrip-
tion of the kinetics of the system and has made MSM a popu-
lar approach for studying protein folding 21–26 and more recently
functional conformational changes of biomolecular machines. 27–40
In practice, the extensive sampling for MSM construction has to
be initiated from conformations obtained by various enhanced
sampling schemes. For protein folding, methods such as par-
allel tempering that enhance sampling in a global manner are
applicable.41,42
For functional conformational changes, however, the motions
to be sampled are often more localized transitions, e.g., protein loop
closure or domain translocation. In these cases, globally enhancing
sampling by raising the temperature may lead to unwanted results
like loss of protein secondary structures. A more feasible strategy is
to generate initial path(s) by methods such as linear interpolation,43
the Climber algorithm,44 targeted MD (tMD),45 or other enhanced
sampling techniques42,46–48 and then optimize the guess path(s) via
path searching methods.49
Unlike path sampling methods, path searching methods, 50,51
represented by the string method,43,52–57 aim to use biased sampling
to locate the minimum free energy path (MFEP) closest to a guess
path. Although only allowing path optimization in a local manner,
the path searching methods are suitable for quickly identifying the
transition mechanism. The MFEP(s) found also ensure the statistical
signiﬁcance and biological relevance of the subsequent unbiased MD
simulations and the construction of MSMs.58–61
Typically, the procedure of path searching methods starts by
specifying a collective variable (CV) space on which the paths are
deﬁned. Sampling initiated from each path node is then performed
for the search. In the string method, the sampling is restrained in
the local volume around each path node in the CV space. 62 A new
path is then obtained by updating the nodes according to the direc-
tion of the mean force 53,54 or the average drift 43 and subsequent
path reparameterization. 62 An iterative procedure as such enables
a gradual downhill evolution of the path, which ﬁnally falls into
the MFEP closest to the initial guess path. This approach has found
great success in characterizing many challenging systems, 58–60,63,64
including huge biomolecular machines.65,66
In practice, however, the overall efﬁciency and the degree of
automation of this approach remain restricted by the local sampling
adopted and the difﬁculty in choosing the static CV space with-
out sufﬁcient prior knowledge of the system. The local sampling
was originally introduced to prevent the path nodes from dropping
quickly to the known free energy minima, without moving in the
directions perpendicular to the current path to search for the actual
transition state (TS) regions. 43,53,54 But it also makes the downhill
evolution very gradual, limiting the speed of the search. In addition,
the a priori choice of the CVs is highly non-trivial, especially for high
dimensional biomolecular systems. In fact, even when initiated from
the same guess path, the string method could ﬁnd distinct MFEPs
in different CV spaces, 54 necessitating post-validation of the found
paths. This sometimes could lead to trial runs of many combina-
tions of CVs, 60,65 potentially diminishing the overall efﬁciency of
the method.
Here we present a new method, Traveling-salesman based
Automated Path Searching (TAPS), to alleviate these issues. We
argue that non-local perpendicular sampling can be pursued to
accelerate the search, provided that the path nodes are reordered
thereafter via a traveling-salesman scheme.67 Moreover, rather than
a point in the reduced CV space, each path node in TAPS is
a high dimensional image of the biomolecular system. By deﬁn-
ing a distance metric, we compute the path collective variables
(PCVs)68 on these high dimensional nodes on-the-ﬂy and use PCV
as a coordinate system in each iteration. Therefore, TAPS naturally
avoids the challenging step of choosing CVs and thus minimizes
the prior knowledge of the system required for the search. Even
when CVs are correctly chosen, for peptide systems (in vacuum
or explicit solvent), TAPS achieves a 5-8 times speedup over the
string method with swarms-of-trajectories (SMwSTs), making it a
promising automated path searching method for large biomolecular
systems.

Note that, unlike the local sampling adopted in the string
method,43,53,54,56 the perpendicular sampling is non-local: although
discouraged to move along the direction of the path by the restrain
potential, the system is allowed to move freely in all directions
perpendicular to the path.
We exemplify such perpendicular sampling on an unphys-
ical path for the C7eq-to-C7ax transition of alanine dipeptide in
vacuum (see the section titled “Simulation Details” and Fig. 2).
Here all heavy atoms of alanine dipeptide are used for structural
alignment and RMSD calculation. Such non-local perpendicular
sampling (1 ps per node in this iteration) can quickly ﬁnd some
segments of the closest MFEP. Note that to facilitate the subse-
quent step of path reparameterization, one dimensional MetaD is
performed on s (not on z), for generating sufﬁcient samples, in
similar spirit to local elevation 89—the immediate predecessor of
MetaD.
Because path nodes typically evolve to regions with lower
free energy during the sampling, the samples collected from such
perpendicular relaxation are natural backbones for a new path.
Since z is unbiased in the sampling around each node, these back-
bones can be found by extracting the centroid of the samples
with the median value of z. However, because the sampling is
non-local, these centroids are often in a wrong order if the node
index in the original path is kept (Fig. 2). Therefore, an automated
scheme to re-generate an order for the median(z) nodes becomes
necessary.

hard,67 when the number of nodes to reorder is <1000, the time for
Concorde to obtain the traveling-salesman path never exceeds a few
minutes on a state-of-the-art desktop computer. Consequently, the
time-cost of this crucial reordering step is negligible compared with
the actual MD sampling.
Path reparameterization
Due to the tendency of the sampling to drop into the known
free energy minima, the nodes of the new path are often unevenly
distributed, necessitating reparameterization of the new path. We
perform the reparameterization via three simple procedures: ter-
minal truncation, nodes insertion, and path straightening. When
multiple parallel MFEPs coexist between the two terminal confor-
mations, the non-local perpendicular sampling is likely to ﬁnd seg-
ments from all these MFEPs, especially in early iterations (see upper
left of the free energy contours in Fig. 2). To focus on the clos-
est MFEP, we ﬁrst ﬁnd the two median(z) nodes closest to the two
known terminal nodes of the original guess path and then disregard
the tail nodes beyond them. Automatic search for parallel MFEPs is
left for future study.
Subsequently, by setting a cut-off distance ∆, we identify the
neighbour nodes with di,i+1 > ∆, select conformations from the
MetaD samples [gray dots in Fig. 3(b)] as new nodes and insert them
between the distant neighbours until di,i+1 ≤ ∆ holds for all neigh-
bour nodes. The selected conformations must be close to the bisec-
tion between the neighbour nodes while holding the largest angle
γ among all bisection conformations [Fig. 3(b)]. To avoid unnec-
essary loops or curves of the path, conformations with γ ≤ 90○ are
never inserted. To further minimize the curvature of the path, we
set a straightening factor σ > 1, short-cut between the nodes with
di,j ≤ σ∆, and then re-insert nodes between them to ensure di,i+1 ≤
∆ for all neighbours [Fig. 3(c)]. Meanwhile, σ must be ≤d1,N/3∆ to
prevent disregarding too many nodes, with d1,N being the distance
between the terminal nodes. Whendi,i+1≤ ∆ cannot be fulﬁlled using
solely the MetaD samples, we generate additional sample confor-

In summary, only four major inputs are required for TAPS: (a)
the atom set for structure alignment, (b) the atom-set for computing
the RMSD and thus the PCV, (c) the cut-off distance ∆ controlling
the path resolution, and (d) the factor σ controlling the straightness

SIMULATION DETAILS
TAPS
All TAPS simulations were performed using GROMACS-
5.0.4,93 PLUMED-2.2.1,94 and Concorde, 90 wrapped within a
python script publicly available at https://github.com/
liusong299/TAPS. All simulations were performed in the NVT
ensemble at T = 300 K, using the velocity-rescale thermostat.95

To test the performance of TAPS on more realistic systems,

for Met-enkephalin is generated by tMD, pulled from a 3 10-helix

all heavy atoms are used for computing the RMSD among the
conformations aligned on all backbone atoms. For SMwST, 28 pair-
wise distances among the heavy atoms are chosen as CVs (Table 1 of
the supplementary material).
We project the paths found by TAPS and SMwST on 3 dis-
tances among different parts of the residues Tyr1 and Met5: C α-
Cα, sidechain-sidechain, and O-N measuring the formation of the
backbone hydrogen bond [Fig. 5(a)]. Clearly, the majority of nodes
in the initial guess path are in regions with high free energy.
Despite minor differences in the ﬁnal paths, TAPS and SMwST
both identify the channel corresponding to the MFEP. In partic-
ular, both methods manage to sample the transition state (TS)
region [Fig. 5(b)]. As validated by committor analysis (Fig. 6),
the TS is a distorted α-helix-like structure, in which the back-
bone hydrogen bond between Met5 and Tyr1 and that between
Met5 and Gly2 are both marginally broken. The corresponding
free energy barrier height is ∼5.2 k BT, as revealed by PCV-metaD
along the SMwST path. These results indicate a critical role of
the backbone hydrogen bond between Met5 and Tyr1 in the
transition.
On average, TAPS needs 3 ns to converge, which is merely
∼1/5 of the 16 ns required for SMwST [Fig. 5(c)]. Here the
lower efﬁciency gains by TAPS than the case of alanine dipep-
tide possibly originate from the different barrier heights for the
two systems: 5.2 k BT for Met-enkephalin and 13 k BT for alanine
dipeptide. A lower barrier could mean easier convergence to the
MFEP and make the difference between the two methods less
pronounced.
DISCUSSION
As an alternative to the string method, 43,53,54 TAPS is par-
ticularly useful as an initial sampling technique for characteriz-
ing the kinetics of large biomolecular systems under the frame-
work of Markov state models 13,14,16,17,19–21,49,108–111 and mile-
stoning.112–116 For instance, the string method has been applied for
such purposes in studying a few signaling proteins. 58–61 The found
that MFEP(s) among the known structures have been used to seed
subsequent unbiased MD simulation for MSM construction. The
higher efﬁciency and the minimal requirement on the prior knowl-
edge of the system of TAPS may make it more suitable for such tasks
than the string method, particularly for large complicated biomolec-
ular systems. We notice that TAPS generates converged paths with
larger ﬂuctuations compared to the string method [see results of ala-
nine dipeptide in Fig. 4(b) and Met-enkephalin in Fig. 5(c)]. This is
not surprising as the extensive unstrained sampling (∼1 ps in TAPS
v.s 0.01 ps in SMwST for alanine dipeptide) along directions per-
pendicular to the path in TAPS could generate substantial sampling
deviated from the path. Accordingly, in TAPS, we obtain the MFEP
by taking the central path ( min/parenleft.alt2∑j/uni27E8z/uni27E9ij/parenright.alt2) among the ones from the
last few rounds.
TAPS can also be considered as an extension to existing meth-
ods that pursued perpendicular sampling, 50,51 not only because of
its avoidance of the static CV space. The sampling in the fast tomo-
graphic method, though perpendicular, is assumed local, possibly
to keep the order of nodes invariant. 51 The reordering scheme in
TAPS avoids this assumption and allows for non-local sampling.
Path-MetaD pursued non-local perpendicular sampling50 but relied
on a single long MetaD simulation. As the sampling of path nodes in
TAPS can be performed simultaneously, TAPS is potentially more
parallelizable than Path-MetaD.
We also note that, for high dimensional systems, RMSD (the
distance metric for deﬁning PCV) could only precisely describe the
similarity between pairs of conformations when its value is small.
Therefore, the cut-off RMSD distance ∆ (see Fig. 2) controlling
the path resolution in TAPS should be set sufﬁciently small. When
the RMSD distance between the initial and ﬁnal conformational
states is large, a small ∆ may inevitably result in a large number of
nodes on the path, slowing down the traveling-salesman reorder-
ing scheme. In such cases, we could limit the number of path nodes
by performing a coarse search (large ∆) for metastable intermedi-
ates that divide the original long path into segments and then high
resolution TAPS runs (small ∆) to locate the MFEP closest to each
segment.
CONCLUSION
We have introduced a highly efﬁcient, parallelizable, and auto-
mated path searching method TAPS for biomolecular systems. It
adopts non-local sampling for a rapid search of segments of the clos-
est MFEP in all directions perpendicular to a guess path and uses
a traveling-salesman scheme to reorder the newly generated path
nodes. Besides, unlike most existing methods, 43,50,51,53,54 TAPS is
based on PCV, deﬁned directly on high dimensional system images.
With such a feature, TAPS minimizes the necessary prior knowledge
about the system and only requires a few major inputs: the atom-sets
for structure alignment and RMSD computation, the cut-off dis-
tance ∆ controlling the path resolution, and the factor σ controlling
the path straightness. Having demonstrated a 5-8 times speedup over
the string method with swarms-of-trajectories, TAPS holds great
promise to be widely applied to large biomolecular systems.
